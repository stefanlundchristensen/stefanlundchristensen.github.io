For the first stretch of my time leading larger organisations, I measured my usefulness by how many problems I personally solved. A stuck migration, a wobbling stakeholder, an incident nobody could untangle: I'd parachute in, sort it out, and feel productive. People even praised it. The word they used was "hands-on," and I wore it like a medal.

The problem with being the hero is arithmetic. Today I run a leadership team of seven who between them lead more than a hundred people. If the organisation's throughput depends on how many rooms I can be in, we top out at my calendar. Everything past that ceiling depends on a different question: how good are the seven, and how good are the people they're growing?

## What the job actually is

It took me embarrassingly long to accept that developing leaders is the job, not a nice-to-have around the edges of it. Not because anyone hid this from me. Every leadership book says it. But the reward loop of solving things yourself is immediate, and the reward loop of growing someone who solves them is measured in quarters. Under pressure, I defaulted to the fast loop more than I'd like to admit.

What changed my behaviour was noticing what happened when I didn't intervene. One of my leads ran a reorganisation of their area that I would have designed differently. I said so once, then stayed out of it. Their design turned out better than mine would have been, because they knew things about their teams that I didn't, and the act of owning it end-to-end changed how they carried themselves in every meeting after. My version would have been fine. Their version made a leader.

## Context, autonomy, support

The frame I've landed on for the seven is three commitments. They get context: the full picture of what the company needs, what the constraints are, what I'm worried about, including the uncomfortable parts I could technically keep to myself. Then real decision space, defined explicitly enough that they don't have to guess where it ends. The third commitment is support, which means that when something is heavy, I help carry it without taking it away from them.

The order matters. Autonomy without context produces confident decisions aimed at the wrong target, and context without autonomy produces analysts rather than leaders. Support is what keeps the first two from burning out exactly the people you can least afford to lose.

The other thing I hold onto: one-to-ones are for development, not status. Status lives in dashboards and written updates, where it belongs. If the only time I spend with a leader is spent reporting, I learn nothing about how they think, and they learn nothing at all.

## The part that costs something

Honesty requires saying what this trade costs. Growing leaders means watching people do things worse than you would, slower than you would, at least at first, while the results carry your name. It means the win gets celebrated in someone else's team channel. And sometimes it means investing a year in someone who then leaves, taking the investment with them. Three people I developed at Pleo went on to become directors, and not all of them report to me anymore. I've decided to count that as the point rather than the loss, but on the wrong day it doesn't feel that way.

I still catch myself reaching for the hero move. An incident channel lights up and my fingers hover over the keyboard, drafting the take-charge message that would feel so productive to send. Most of the time now, I close the draft and watch one of the seven handle it, differently than I would have, and usually well. I keep learning the same lesson on a loop: the organisation I want to build is one that makes me unnecessary, and every day I act indispensable is a day I've built the opposite.
