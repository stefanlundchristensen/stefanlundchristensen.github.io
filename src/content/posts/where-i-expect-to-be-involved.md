A leader who once reported to me described my style as being "clear on where he expects to be involved and on what I can make the decisions myself." I'd like to claim this was always a design. It wasn't. It emerged from years of getting delegation wrong in both directions: hovering over decisions I should have released, and being surprised by decisions I assumed would come to me.

The failure that finally made me write it down was a market launch where a lead made a perfectly reasonable vendor commitment, one I would have vetoed for reasons they had no way of knowing about, involving a partnership discussion happening two levels up. Their judgment was fine. What failed was my framework, which consisted in its entirety of "use your judgment," operating on information I'd never shared.

Since then, every senior person who joins my team gets an explicit conversation in their first weeks, and a written note after it. The format is simple enough to fit on a page.

## The three lists

**Yours alone.** Decisions the person makes without me, and without informing me beyond normal reporting. Team structure inside their area, technical choices within the agreed architecture, hiring against approved headcount, prioritisation inside their committed goals. The test for this list: if I second-guess one of these decisions after the fact, that's my failure, not theirs. Saying that sentence out loud, and meaning it, is what makes the list real.

**Yours, but tell me.** Decisions they own where I want to hear the outcome promptly, because I may need to carry the context elsewhere: to the executive team, to a partner, to a regulator. Changes affecting another department's plans. Anything that moves a committed date. Personnel decisions above a certain seniority. The commitment on my side is that "tell me" means tell me, and never quietly turns into "ask me."

**Bring me in early.** The short list where I expect to be in the room before the decision is made, either because the blast radius crosses my whole area or because I hold context that cannot be fully shared downward: regulatory exposure, board-level commitments, anything that touches the company's licence to operate. ## What makes it work

**The lists are asymmetric on purpose.** The first list should be the longest by far, and it should grow every quarter. If it doesn't, either I'm not developing the person or I'm not releasing my grip, and both are on me.

**The reasons travel with the list.** Every item in the second and third lists comes with the why. "Bring me in on scheme relationships because I'm accountable to the regulator for them" lands differently than an unexplained boundary, and it lets the person predict where the boundary will sit in situations the lists never anticipated. That prediction ability is the actual goal. The lists are training data.

**It gets revisited, not just written.** We re-walk the lists roughly twice a year, and after any surprise in either direction. A surprise means the model and reality have drifted, and the document, not the person, gets corrected.

**It survives pressure only if I do.** The whole system is tested the first time something on the "yours alone" list goes wrong publicly. If I respond by pulling the decision back, everyone learns the lists were decorative. The discipline is fixing the outcome together, then asking whether the list was wrong, and usually concluding it wasn't.

I've watched this one conversation change how quickly a new leader gets fast. The alternative, calibrating by trial and error against an unspoken model in my head, wastes months and burns trust in both directions. Write the lists down. The half day it takes is the cheapest leadership investment I know of, and I learned its price by paying the expensive version first.
