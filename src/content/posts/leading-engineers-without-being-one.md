I have never held a job as a software engineer. No bootcamp, no computer science degree, no years in the trenches shipping features. My training is in physics, my apprenticeship was in consulting, and yet for most of a decade my job has been leading engineers, at this point a lot of them.

Early on, this felt like a secret I was keeping. Engineering leadership culture carries a strong assumption that authority flows from having done the job: you earn the right to lead deploys by having broken production yourself, years ago, at 3am. I'd sit in architecture reviews quietly running the math on when someone would ask a question that exposed me.

## What I did instead of pretending

The obvious failure mode for a non-engineer leading engineers is bluffing, and the second most obvious is its mirror image: retreating into "I'll leave the technical stuff to the team" and managing purely by spreadsheet. I tried the second one briefly. It was a disaster in slow motion, because you cannot weigh trade-offs you refuse to understand, and engineers can smell a leader who nods along.

What worked was simpler and more uncomfortable: asking the questions a smart outsider asks, out loud, until the system made sense. Walk me through what happens to this payment after the customer taps the card. Why does this migration need a year, what specifically eats the time? What would have to be true for this to fall over at ten times the volume? A physics education turns out to be decent preparation for this. Not because quantum mechanics comes up in payment processing, but because a PhD is five years of being the person in the room who doesn't understand yet, and learning to ask your way out of it without shame.

Something unexpected happened as I did this. Engineers didn't lose respect for me when I asked basic questions. They lost respect when I pretended to know things I didn't. The questions themselves often did useful work: the third "why" in a row would occasionally reveal that nobody in the room knew, and that we'd all been standing on an assumption.

## Judgment is the actual job

Over time I stopped thinking of my background as a gap to compensate for and started noticing what the role actually demands. Nobody needs me to write the code. They need someone who can hold the whole system in their head, connect it to what the business needs, make decisions under uncertainty, and take accountability for how it turns out. Deep technical problem-solving, it turns out, is a different skill from programming: decomposing a problem until the hard part is exposed. I'd been training that muscle since the atomic clock lab.

There are real limits, and pretending otherwise would undercut the point. I will never review a pull request for correctness, and I've learned to be explicit about where my judgment ends and my leads' begins. I hire senior engineers partly as a check on myself, and I tell them so. When a decision is purely about implementation, my job is to make sure the right people are deciding, not to have an opinion.

## For everyone leading outside their training

More people lead outside their training than admit it: the product leader running data scientists, the operations executive who inherited a security function, the founder managing a discipline they've never practiced. The credibility they're worried about comes less from the résumé line they're missing than from curiosity that's visibly real, questions that make the work sharper, and a track record of decisions that turned out well.

Fifteen years in, engineers who once made me nervous now tell me the depth of technical problem-solving is what they remember from working together. What I wish I'd believed sooner is that the room needs you to make everyone in it better, which nobody issues a degree for, and which has nothing to do with being the best engineer standing there.
